What the service sees

One message, three places

It is sealed before it leaves you and opened after it arrives. In between, the thing that carries it is holding what it cannot read.

The key that opens it is made on your computers and stays there. The service relays and stores the middle column, and that is all it ever has.

FAQ

Questions, answered

Is Callifornia really free?

Yes. There's no subscription, no trial timer, and no features locked behind a paywall. You download it, sign in, and call people.

Which platforms are supported?

Windows 10 and 11 (64 bit), and Linux (.deb for Debian and Ubuntu, .rpm for Fedora and RHEL). The Linux build is younger than the Windows one, so it keeps a beta badge while it settles. macOS is planned, and since the app's core is already cross platform it's a matter of packaging rather than a rewrite.

Windows warned me about an unrecognized app. Why?

That's Microsoft SmartScreen being cautious about new publishers. The installer isn't signed with a paid certificate yet, so Windows shows a generic warning for it. Click "More info", then "Run anyway".

Signing is on our roadmap, and once it is in place the warning goes away.

Do I need an account?

Yes, a lightweight one. You sign up with an email address and a password, confirm the address with a code, and your username is made from that address. The username is what friends search for, and you can change it, your display name and your photo later. The address is there for password resets and for letting a new computer in, not for a mailing list.

Why does a second computer ask me for a code?

Because a password on its own cannot tell you apart from somebody who has your password. A computer this account has not used before signs in but opens nothing until one you already use reads an eight character code off it. Typing that code proves somebody is sitting at both computers. When there is no other computer left, a code goes to your email instead, the way a password reset does.

What happens if I lose my computer?

Your account comes back by email. What was said before does not, because it is locked with a key that lived on the computer you no longer have, and the service never had a copy. Resetting a forgotten password has the same effect, while changing a password you still know keeps everything, since the key was never tied to it. The table in the privacy policy lays out every case.

How do updates work?

On Windows, the app checks for new versions in the background, downloads only the files that changed, and verifies a cryptographic signature on every update before applying it, so you restart once and you're done. On Linux, updates ship through a signed APT and YUM repository instead, so the app tells you one is available and your package manager installs it (one click from inside the app if it can ask for your password, otherwise a regular apt upgrade/dnf upgrade). See what's changed in the changelog.

What data does Callifornia collect?

What you say is not part of it. Messages and call media are sealed on your computer, so the service holds ciphertext it cannot open. What it does hold is your address, your profile, who your friends are, and when and how long you called, which is metadata and worth knowing about. Calls are never recorded, and crash reports are strictly voluntary and off by default. The privacy policy spells out both halves.
